Korean sentence order and particles, explained through Hindi

Here is good news that most Korean courses, written for English speakers, never mention: Korean grammar is built much like Hindi grammar. The verb comes last. Small markers follow the noun instead of coming before it. Respect is shown through word endings. If you think in Hindi, Punjabi, Bangla, Tamil or almost any Indian language, Korean sentence order will feel natural in a way it never does for an English speaker.

1. The verb comes last

LanguageSentenceOrder
EnglishI eat rice.subject, verb, object
Hindiमैं चावल खाता हूँ।subject, object, verb
Korean저는 밥을 먹어요. (jeoneun babeul meogeoyo)subject, object, verb

Translate a Korean sentence word by word into Hindi and it usually stays in order. Translate it into English and you must turn it around.

2. Particles work like Hindi postpositions

English puts small words before the noun: in the house, from Delhi. Hindi puts them after: घर में, दिल्ली से. Korean does the same, and it joins them to the noun.

ParticleJobHindiExample

에to; at (place, time)को, में, पर학교에 가요 I go to school
에서at (doing something); fromमें, से집에서 먹어요 I eat at home

와 / 과, 하고and; withऔर, के साथ친구하고 with a friend

Where two forms are given, the first follows a consonant and the second follows a vowel: 책은 (chaek + eun) but 저는 (jeo + neun). The choice only makes the word easier to say.

3. Topic and subject: the one hard part

Korean marks the "topic" of a sentence (은/는) differently from the plain subject (이/가). English has nothing like it, but Hindi's "तो" comes close.

  • 저는 학생이에요. "As for me, I am a student." (मैं तो छात्र हूँ।) You are introducing or contrasting yourself.
  • 제가 학생이에요. "I am the student." (छात्र मैं हूँ।) You are answering "who is the student?"

As a beginner's rule: use 은/는 for what you are talking about in general, and 이/가 for something new, or with 있어요 (there is) and 없어요 (there is not). You will be right most of the time, and nobody will misunderstand you when you are not.

4. Words that can be left out

Korean drops whatever is obvious. "I", "you" and even the object vanish if the situation makes them clear. 밥 먹었어요? is literally "rice ate?", and it means "Have you eaten?" Spoken Hindi does this too: "खाना खाया?" In casual speech the particles themselves are often dropped.

5. No articles, no gender, no plural agreement

There is no "a" or "the". Nouns have no gender, so there is nothing like खाता / खाती to remember. The verb does not change for I, you or they. A plural marker 들 exists but is used only when needed.

6. "To be" and "to have"

  • 이에요 / 예요 = is, am, are, joined to a noun: 학생이에요 "(I) am a student".
  • 있어요 = there is; I have. 없어요 = there is not; I do not have. 시간이 없어요, "there is no time", is just like Hindi "समय नहीं है": both languages say "to me, time is not" instead of "I have".

Build ten sentences now

Take a place, an object and a verb from our word lists and fill this frame: [I]는 [place]에서 [thing]을/를 [verb]. For example: 저는 집에서 김밥을 먹어요. "I eat gimbap at home." Change one word at a time. This single frame, practised well, is worth a chapter of grammar.
