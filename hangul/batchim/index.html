Batchim: final consonants and how sounds change

Batchim (받침) means "support" or "prop". It is the consonant written at the bottom of a syllable block, holding the block up from below: the ㄴ in 한, the ㅂ in 밥.

Here is the surprise. Although almost any consonant can be written as a batchim, at the end of a word only seven sounds are actually spoken: k, n, t, l, m, p and ng. Korean does not release a final consonant with a puff of air as English does. The mouth closes and stops. So several different letters end up with the same sound. ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ and ㄷ are all pronounced as an unreleased "t" at the end of a word. 옷 (clothes) is written with s but said "ot".

The original consonant is not lost, however. It comes back the moment a vowel follows, as you will see in the linking rule below. This is why Korean spelling keeps the "real" letter: the spelling shows the word, and the rules give the sound.

The linking rule, slowly

When a block ends in a consonant and the next block begins with the silent ㅇ, the consonant slides over and fills the empty place. 한국어 (the Korean language) is written han-guk-eo and spoken han-gu-geo. 음악 (music) is written eum-ak and spoken eu-mak. Hindi readers do something similar without thinking when words run together in speech. Once you start linking, your Korean suddenly sounds smooth instead of chopped.

Why "thank you" is not said the way it is spelt

감사합니다 is spelt gam-sa-hap-ni-da, but everyone says gam-sa-ham-ni-da. The ㅂ (p) stands just before ㄴ (n), and it is easier for the mouth to turn p into its nasal twin m. The same thing happens in every polite verb ending in -ㅂ니다, so you will hear this rule in almost every formal sentence. The official romanisation follows the sound, which is why this site writes "gamsahamnida".

Double batchim

A few words have two consonants at the bottom, as in 읽다 (to read), 없다 (to not exist) and 앉다 (to sit). Before a consonant or at the end, only one of the two is spoken. Before a vowel, the second one links across: 읽어요 is said il-geo-yo, and 없어요 is said eop-seo-yo. Do not try to memorise the full table now. Learn each such word with its sound when you meet it.

How much of this does a beginner need?

Three things: the seven final sounds, the linking rule, and the p-to-m change in -ㅂ니다. With those, you can read aloud almost everything on this site correctly. The other rules will settle in through listening. Use the Listen button on the phrase pages and compare what you hear with the spelling.
