Korean for Hindi speakers: what is similar and what is different

Most Korean courses are written for English speakers and spend chapters on things that an Indian learner already has for free. Korean and Hindi are not related languages. They come from different families and share no common vocabulary apart from a few words that reached both from other sources. Yet they are built on strikingly similar lines. Knowing where they match, and where they do not, saves months.

What you already have

1. Word order

Both languages put the verb at the end: मैं स्कूल जाता हूँ and 저는 학교에 가요 line up word for word. Describing words come before the noun in both, and so do longer descriptions: "कल ख़रीदी हुई किताब" and 어제 산 책 ("yesterday-bought book").

2. Postpositions

में, से, को, तक, भी follow the noun; so do 에, 에서, 을/를, 까지, 도. An English speaker has to relearn where small words go. You do not. See the full table in sentence order and particles.

3. Levels of respect

We grow up choosing between तू, तुम, आप and changing the verb to match. Korean's speech levels are the same habit of mind. So is calling unrelated people भैया, दीदी, अंकल, आंटी, which matches 형, 언니, 아저씨, 아줌마, and adding जी to a name, which matches 씨 and 님.

4. Aspirated and unaspirated sounds

Hindi keeps क and ख, प and फ apart. Korean's ㄲ and ㅋ, ㅃ and ㅍ differ in much the same way, a difference English speakers can barely hear.

5. "Do" verbs and compound verbs

Hindi makes verbs from nouns with करना: काम करना, पढ़ाई करना. Korean does it with 하다: 일하다, 공부하다. Hindi says "दे दो", "कर लो" with a helper verb; Korean says 해 주세요 ("do-give", please do it for me) the same way.

6. Dropping the obvious

"खाना खाया?" "हाँ, खा लिया।" No "you", no "I". Korean: 밥 먹었어요? 네, 먹었어요. Exactly the same economy.

7. Echo words and sound words

Hindi loves doubled words such as धीरे-धीरे, टप-टप, चमचम. Korean is famous for them: 천천히 (slowly), 반짝반짝 (twinkle-twinkle), 두근두근 (the thump of a heart). They are fun to learn and fill the pages of web novels.

What is easier than Hindi

  • No grammatical gender. Nothing like अच्छा / अच्छी or गया / गई.
  • No verb agreement. One verb form for I, you, he, she, we and they.
  • A smaller alphabet. 24 basic letters and no half-letter conjuncts.
  • No "ने" rule. Past sentences do not change the subject's marker.

What is truly new

  • Two vowels: ㅓ (eo) and especially ㅡ (eu). See the vowels page.
  • No retroflex sounds, and fewer consonants. Korean has no ट, ड, ण, no voiced aspirates like घ, ध, भ, and no f, v or z. Your name may change a little in Hangul.
  • Topic versus subject (은/는 and 이/가). Hindi's "तो" helps, but it takes practice.
  • Two number systems, each with its own uses. See numbers.
  • Sound changes between syllables. See batchim.
  • Vocabulary. This is the real work. Apart from English loanwords, almost every word is new, and there is no shortcut except regular reading and listening.

How to use this advantage

  1. Translate into Hindi, not English, when you study a Korean sentence. The order will hold, and the grammar will make sense.
  2. Map each particle to a Hindi postposition on a single card and keep it by your desk.
  3. Trust your ear for aspiration. When a textbook says the three k's are hard to tell apart, remember that it was written for someone else.
  4. Spend the time you save on vocabulary. Ten new words a day, every day.

A note of honesty: this comparison is meant as a practical learning aid. It does not claim that the two languages are related. Linguists place Hindi in the Indo-European family; the family of Korean is still debated, and it is often treated as standing on its own.
